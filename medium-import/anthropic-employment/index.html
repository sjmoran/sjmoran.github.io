What Anthropic’s Workforce Data Says About AI at Work

An AI lab measured itself: more output per person, fewer junior hires, and human attention as the new constraint. The direction of each finding holds up better than the size.

In October, Anthropic’s economics team published How employment is changing at Anthropic (Wilmers, Catipon, Massenkoff and McCrory, 2026). It uses internal data that almost no outside researcher could get: headcount, agent telemetry, code commits, calendars, Slack threads, survey responses and interview scorecards for thousands of hires, plus 17 employee interviews.

In early 2025, 27% of Anthropic’s new technical hires were junior, meaning the lower bands of its levelling scale. By mid-2026 the figure was 9%. Over the same period the company tripled in size and its employees moved much of their work onto AI agents.

I wanted to know what a practising data scientist should take from the report. That means two things: what it shows about how AI-heavy work is organised, and how far its measurements support the stronger claims. My answer is that the organisational findings are the most useful part, and the productivity estimates show a real effect whose size is still uncertain.

Takeaways

  • Output per person rose in every function the report measured. The evidence is strongest for engineers, where a within-person estimate is available, and even that can’t rule out selection into adoption.
  • Human attention is now the main constraint. In interactive sessions, where a person directs an agent turn by turn, agents spend about 80% of their time idle, and their output would take non-technical staff more than half a working week to read.
  • A growing share of the work is “meta-work”: writing specs, instructions and monitors for agents. It rose from 10% to 22% of interactive sessions in six months.
  • Hiring shifted toward judgement and autonomy, away from domain expertise and execution. Junior technical hiring fell sharply.
  • Human decisions that aren’t written down become invisible to agents, so keeping a written record becomes an operational requirement.

Fewer junior roles in a company that tripled

The employment findings are about shares. Technical hiring kept rising in absolute terms; what changed is the mix. Juniors fell from 27% to 9% of new technical hires, and from 21% to 14% of headcount in technical departments. Postings for junior roles fell most sharply from December 2025 onwards.

By January 2026, the teams that used Claude most heavily were saying openly that writing code and finding bugs could be handed off. One manager said the hiring needs changed “basically overnight” once a newer model gave performance engineers a large uplift. In an April survey, about a third of ML managers and a fifth of software engineering managers said they were hiring less because of AI.

Inside technical departments, more people now write code. Over the year to April 2026, the group who weren’t actively coding (no commits in the previous 30 days) shrank by almost half as a share of technical staff, and managers’ commits doubled. Supervisory roles now involve shipping code. Outside technical departments, administrative headcount barely grew as the company scaled, because agents took over scheduling, email triage and purchase-request review.

A chart across job titles connects the hiring decline to AI use. The authors scored each job title by how complex its Claude tasks were in late 2025, before junior hiring fell. Between January and July 2026, junior headcount grew least in the job titles with the most complex use. This fits the idea that AI substitutes for junior work first: where seniors already hand the model hard tasks, the easier tasks juniors used to learn on go to the model too.

The productivity estimate, and what it identifies

The aggregate numbers are large. Code commits per engineer are up about fivefold in six months, and lawyers, accounts payable staff and salespeople all processed more work. The company’s revenue grew very fast over the same months, though, and selling a strong model makes deals easier to close. The aggregate trend can’t separate AI from a business boom.

The authors handle this with a staggered-adoption design. They follow 192 engineers who adopted coding agents and 74 who didn’t, with person and calendar-month fixed effects, and date adoption from each engineer’s first month with at least five agent sessions. Calendar-month effects absorb anything that hit everyone at once, such as a new model or a revenue surge. Person effects absorb stable differences in ability. This design measures lines of code added rather than commits, so its results aren’t directly comparable with the fivefold figure. Lines added rise sharply in the months after adoption. The report presents this as an event-study chart, with coefficients by month since adoption, rather than as one headline number.

The first limit is that adoption isn’t random. An engineer about to start a large project has a reason both to adopt tools and to write more code. The report’s own evidence points the same way: in a separate analysis of fully autonomous agents (ones that run without a person approving each step), engineers who adopted one were already on a steep upward trend before they switched. The report’s text doesn’t say whether the main estimate’s pre-adoption coefficients are flat, and that is the first thing I’d check on its chart.

The second is the estimator. Two-way fixed effects with staggered timing has known problems when effects differ across people or grow over time. Engineers who adopted early end up in the comparison group for later adopters, even though their own output is still climbing because of the agents. When the effect grows over time, those comparisons distort the average, and in bad cases they can flip its sign (Goodman-Bacon, 2021; Callaway and Sant’Anna, 2021; Sun and Abraham, 2021). Agent effects probably do grow, since every model release raises them. Those papers propose estimators that compare each group of adopters only with engineers who haven’t adopted yet, and I’d want to see one before trusting the magnitude.

The third is the outcome measure. Agents write more lines for the same function, so lines added overstates output. The authors partly address this, and their quality check is the most convincing comparison in the report. They compare code written by an agent with code written directly by the same person, in the same month, in the same directory. On deletion within 90 days, bug-fix deletions, reverts, lint suppressions, “needs fixing” notes and skipped tests, they find no difference. Holding person, month and directory fixed removes most of the obvious confounders. Deletion rates are still a partial view of quality: AI errors may be harder to spot, and code nobody reviews closely also tends not to get deleted.

For non-engineers the evidence is softer, and the same selection problem applies. Sales reps closed deals with more clients after adopting agents than before. The interviews make the mechanism plausible: one account executive said automations took over about 20% of their prior tasks, and another reached 30% more customers. I’d put most weight on the engineering result and treat the rest as consistent with it.

Human attention is the constraint

Agents in interactive sessions spend about 80% of wall-clock time idle, waiting for a person to respond. The authors also convert everything Claude wrote in those sessions into reading time, at 0.75 words per token and 250 words per minute. For non-technical staff, the output of an employee’s own sessions would take more than half of that employee’s 40-hour week to read. Nobody reads a diff at prose speed, so this measures how much output lands on a person rather than time actually spent, but it puts token volume in the unit that matters here: a person’s working hours.

Employees describe this as a prioritisation problem as much as a volume problem. One engineer called it “an army of Claudes, with every one asking for my attention”. Another compared it to babysitting: every child asks with the same urgency, whether the problem is an unopened juice box or a cut finger.

Employees respond by building systems around the agents. Standing instructions, the written files that tell an agent how to behave on every task, grew across the company from a few hundred in July 2025 to 8,500 a year later. Meta-work rose from 10% to 22% of interactive sessions between autumn 2025 and spring 2026. The report counts as meta-work any session spent writing reusable instructions for agents, specs for multi-agent runs, one agent reviewing another, or monitors for agent pipelines. That share comes from an automated classifier run over 2,000 one-line session summaries, with no validation against human labels reported, so I’d trust the direction and rough size of the rise more than the exact percentages.

Data scientists already do this work. Writing a spec an agent can execute and defining what “done” looks like are evaluation design, and building a dashboard that flags pipeline drift is monitoring. The report says data scientists are querying company data more than before, largely to feed real-time metrics to decision-makers. I expect the share of our time spent checking whether output can be trusted to keep growing.

Agents need written context, and that changes how people work

Meeting time grew alongside agent use. The median employee spends 20% more time in meetings than a year ago, and technical individual contributors 16% more. Employees said face-to-face discussion is needed when a bug turns out to come from tech debt, unclear ownership or an organisational decision, none of which an agent can settle.

A decision made in a meeting with no written record is invisible to agents, which then keep working from outdated assumptions. One employee whose name was on a months-old document about company merchandise kept getting agent requests for t-shirts long after changing roles. The company’s response is a default toward doing work in the open, in channels agents can read.

The Slack analysis gives this some structure. Agents appeared in nearly half of active public channels, but mostly in dedicated channels for talking to Claude. Human threads kept the same number of human replies and added agent replies on top. So agents mostly act as private helpers to one employee each, and they see only what that employee writes down.

What hiring managers now look for

The authors ran a classifier over interview scorecards for thousands of hires since 2024. In 2024, interviewers mentioned a candidate’s creative use of AI tools in about 5% of technical assessments and 7% of non-technical ones. By 2026 the technical rate had tripled and the non-technical rate had quadrupled. Like the meta-work classifier, this one has no reported validation against human labels. For technical candidates the biggest gains were in comments about autonomy and judgement, while domain expertise shrank as a share of assessment. For non-technical candidates, customer and mission orientation rose and execution skills fell.

One manager’s reason is a forecast about models rather than people: “I expect models to continue to be jagged, so for my team I want them to be fungible across domains.” If model ability is uneven and shifts with every release, the valuable person is the one who can move to wherever the model is currently weak.

My view, which the report doesn’t test, is that judgement is mostly built by doing the execution work that agents now take. The report notes that some employees are “increasingly removed from the object-level work that originally built their skills”. So hiring selects for judgement while the work removes the usual way to acquire it.

Limits worth keeping in view

Anthropic is an unusual firm to generalise from. It sells the tool being studied, it grew very quickly, and its employees hold stock, so they gain directly when adoption succeeds and have more reason than most staff to automate their own tasks. In an August survey, its staff expected AI to be able to do 75% of their work by 2030; the report puts the figure for other Americans at 28%. A workforce that optimistic probably adopts faster than most, so other firms may see slower uptake. Its recommendations (shared ownership, an early-warning system for at-risk roles, internal redeployment) are framed as one employer’s choices, and none of them help people who were never hired, which is where the junior decline falls.

The report’s most quoted figure needs care too: by August, the agents employees ran in interactive sessions logged six times as many hours as the employees themselves. Agent hours count runtime, including inference and tool use, summed across parallel agents, and are compared with a flat 40-hour human week. Ten agents running for an hour count as ten hours. The ratio tracks how fast agent use grew; it says little about how effort divides between people and agents.

What I’d take from it

For a data science team, two things are worth doing now. Measure the review bottleneck directly, for example agent idle time and the backlog of unreviewed output, because output per person will look good while that backlog grows. And keep decisions in places agents can read.

The analysis I’d most like to see next is a follow-up on the engineers hired as juniors in 2025, to learn whether they build judgement at the rate earlier cohorts did without doing as much of the execution work.
