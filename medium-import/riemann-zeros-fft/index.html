Finding the Riemann Zeros in a Fourier Transform of the Primes

An intuitive guide for data scientists to the Riemann hypothesis and the main ideas behind the new proof, using signals, spectra and covariance matrices.

I counted every prime up to a trillion, turned the counting error into a signal, and took its Fourier transform. The spectrum has 79 sharp peaks below frequency 200. Each peak sits within 0.03 of a zero of the Riemann zeta function, and none of the 79 zeros in that range is missing.

Those zeros are what the Riemann hypothesis is about. The hypothesis was stated in 1859. On 20 November 2026 Levent Alpöge announced a proof, written in Lean, a programming language in which a small trusted program checks every step of a proof. The next day the maintainers of Lean and its mathematics library, Mathlib, certified it [2].

The proof is 17 million lines long. The steps below build up what the hypothesis says and how the proof works, using ideas a data scientist already knows. The mathematics in Steps 1 to 4 is not new; most of it goes back to Riemann. By the end you should be able to explain four things:

  • What the zeros are. They are the frequencies of a signal made from the primes.
  • What the hypothesis says. None of those waves grows in size over time.
  • Why it reduces to one inequality. A signal that never rises above a ceiling cannot contain a growing wave.
  • How the proof gets that inequality. It finds a quantity that can never be negative, and then sharpens it step by step.

Step 1: the primes make a signal

Let us start with counting. There are 25 primes up to 100, 78,498 up to a million, and 37,607,912,018 up to a trillion. The count grows smoothly on average, and there is a good formula for that average, called li(X). Up to a million, li(X) predicts 78,628 primes, so it is off by 130. Up to a trillion it is off by about 38,000.

Suppose we compare those errors with √X. At a million, √X is 1,000; at a trillion it is a million. In both cases the error is well below √X. This is the size of error you would expect from a sum of X random coin flips, so in this sense the primes behave like noise.

To study the error as a signal, we make three small changes:

  • We weight and smooth the count. Each prime p counts as log p instead of 1, which makes the average trend simply X. We also smooth the running total, so that small jumps average out. The resulting error is called E(X); its formula is in the last section.
  • We divide by √X. This keeps the signal at a constant size.
  • We use log X as the time axis. We will write t for log X.

The result is a time series that I will call the prime signal.

The prime signal looks like stationary noise. It wiggles around zero, with no trend and no growing variance.

Step 2: each zero is one wave in the signal

The zeta function is a sum over all whole numbers:

Euler showed in 1737 that the same function can be written as a product with one factor per prime [4]. This works because every whole number breaks into primes in exactly one way. So zeta is a compact way of storing the primes.

A zero of zeta is a value of s where ζ(s) = 0. The interesting zeros are complex numbers, and they all lie in a strip where the real part is between 0 and 1. A complex number has two parts, so each zero is a point in that strip. We will write a zero as a + iγ, where a is its position from left to right and γ is its height.

Each zero puts one wave into the prime signal, and its two parts control that wave:

  • The height γ is the wave’s frequency. The zero at height 14.13 gives a wave that repeats every 2π/14.13 ≈ 0.44 units of t.
  • The position a sets the wave’s growth. The wave grows at rate a − ½ in t. At a = ½ it keeps a constant size; at a = 0.6 it would grow.

If you have used an FFT, this will feel familiar. A zero at a + iγ contributes X raised to the power a + iγ to the counting error. After dividing by √X, that term is exp((a − ½)t) times exp(iγt): a growth factor times a rotating phasor, the same phasor an FFT uses. The connection to the primes is direct. The zeros of ζ are the poles of ζ′/ζ, the derivative of ζ divided by ζ, and the coefficients of ζ′/ζ are exactly the prime weights. So the zeros play the role that poles play in a transfer function, each one contributing a mode with its own frequency and growth rate.

Riemann’s explicit formula makes this exact [3, 5]. It says that the prime signal is the sum of these waves, one per zero, with amplitude 2/(¼ + γ²):

We can test this in two directions. First, adding up the waves from the zeros should rebuild the prime signal. It does: with the first 3 zeros the rebuild has correlation 0.94 with the real signal, and with the first 100 zeros the correlation is 0.9998.

Second, the Fourier transform of the prime signal should have a peak at each zero’s height. That is Figure 1. To make it readable I multiply the spectrum by ¼ + γ², which undoes the shrinking amplitudes, and I apply a Hann window to reduce leakage. With primes up to a trillion the time axis is 23.7 units long, which is enough to separate zeros about 0.5 apart. The closest pair below 200 is 0.72 apart, so every zero appears as its own peak.

Step 3: the Riemann hypothesis says no wave grows

We can now state the hypothesis in one sentence. The Riemann hypothesis says that every zero has a = ½. All the zeros lie on one vertical line, called the critical line, and so every wave in the prime signal keeps a constant size forever.

Suppose instead a single zero had a = 0.51. Its wave would grow slowly, the prime signal would gain a component that gets steadily louder, and the series would stop being stationary.

Data alone cannot settle this, because a bad zero could sit high up. Take a zero at height one million with a = 0.51. Its wave starts with amplitude about 2 × 10⁻¹² and grows by a factor of e every 100 units of t. It would become visible only around X = 10¹⁰³⁶, a number with over a thousand digits. Computers have checked that the first ten trillion zeros all lie on the line [10], but no amount of checking covers all of them.

Step 4: one inequality is enough

A wave of constant size spends half its time above zero and half below. A wave whose size keeps growing will eventually rise above any ceiling you choose.

So suppose we could prove a ceiling: the prime signal stays below some constant C for all large X. Then the signal cannot contain a growing wave, and no zero can have a above ½. Process monitoring uses the same logic. A metric that never breaches its upper control limit cannot contain an oscillation whose swings keep growing. Edmund Landau made this argument rigorous in 1905 [6]. It needs care because there are infinitely many waves, and it relies on the gap between the ceiling and the signal never being negative.

That handles zeros to the right of the line. Zeros to the left are handled by symmetry. Every zero has a mirror image across the critical line, so a zero on the left would come with one on the right, and the ceiling has already ruled that out.

Riemann derived the mirror symmetry from a sum of Gaussians, using the fact that a Gaussian keeps its shape under the Fourier transform. That sum of Gaussians is called a theta function. It will come back in the proof.

The Riemann hypothesis is therefore equivalent to a single ceiling on the prime signal. Figure 2 shows the ceiling holding up to a trillion. The proof has to show it holds for every large X.

Step 5: the proof in four moves

Alpöge’s proof gets the ceiling in four moves. This section is my reading of his remark [1] and of the structure of the Lean files. I traced the files but did not rebuild the hardest step, so treat the account below as an outline of the argument rather than a check of it.

Move 1: reduce. This is Step 4. The proof works with exactly the smoothed signal we have been plotting; it appears in the Lean files under the name Gz. The proof also does more than the Riemann hypothesis. It proves the same statement for a whole family of related functions, the Dirichlet L-functions, and the Riemann hypothesis is the simplest case.

Move 2: positivity. A quantity that can never be negative is a cheap source of one-sided bounds. Variances are the standard example: if you can write something as a variance, you get an inequality for free.

The finite version of the problem shows how this works. Do arithmetic modulo a prime p, like a clock with p hours, and count the solutions of an equation such as y² = f(x). These counts play the role of the prime counts. Instead of infinitely many zeros they have only four, α₁ to α₄, and the hypothesis says each has size √p. André Weil proved it in the 1940s [7].

Here the hypothesis is a covariance check. Scale the α’s by √p and add up their r-th powers:

The four α’s come in two complex-conjugate pairs. If every scaled α has size 1, c(r) is the autocovariance of a time series made of two sinusoids. Its covariance matrix, with entry c(i − j) in row i and column j, is then a sum of outer products, and so it has no negative eigenvalues. If an α is off the circle, a large enough matrix has a negative eigenvalue. I checked nine curves: every matrix up to 29 × 29 had smallest eigenvalue no lower than −2 × 10⁻¹³, which is rounding error. Moving the α’s off the circle by a factor of 1.2 gave −656.

Weil showed that the same kind of test exists for the primes [8]. The Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to a certain quadratic form, built from the primes, never being negative. With infinitely many zeros, though, there is no finite matrix to check. Alpöge’s remark describes proving this positivity for chosen test functions “via a determinant”. A determinant of this kind measures the volume spanned by a set of vectors, and a squared volume is never negative. My reading of the remark is that this positivity already gives a weak ceiling, which rules out zeros with a close to 1.

Move 3: multiplicity. The weak ceiling is sharpened using an idea from the finite version. In 1969 Stepanov proved the curve case without Weil’s heavy machinery [9]. His method rests on a budget argument. A polynomial of degree d has a budget of d roots. If it touches zero with multiplicity 10 at every point you are counting, each point costs 10 roots, so there can be at most d/10 points. The hard part is building a polynomial of low degree that vanishes to high order at all the points at once.

Alpöge’s remark says the proof gets its multiplicity from theta lifts. A theta lift is a kernel transform built from theta functions, the sums of Gaussians from Step 4, in many dimensions:

You can think of it as multiplying by a kernel matrix that moves a function from one space of symmetries to a larger one. The remark says each lift “wins multiplicity”, and that the number of lifts grows with the scale X being studied. My reading is that the positive quantity from Move 2 is made to vanish to higher and higher order, one lift at a time, and that each extra order tightens the ceiling in the same way that it sharpens Stepanov’s count.

Move 4: bootstrap. Each round of the argument turns a ceiling into a zero-free region, meaning no zeros with a above some value. A better zero-free region then feeds back into a sharper ceiling. The remark describes this loop as a bootstrap, and according to it, repeated rounds bring the region all the way to the line a = ½, which is the Riemann hypothesis.

What the picture does not explain

The Fourier picture explains what the zeros are, what the hypothesis says, and why one inequality is enough. Moves 2 to 4 give the shape of the proof. It does not explain why the multiplicity from the theta lifts builds up fast enough, and why the errors stay under control across so many lifts. Those steps are where the 17 million lines of Lean do their work, and I could not rebuild them from the remark.

If you want to try the spectrum yourself, the code below reproduces it up to frequency 60 for primes up to ten million, in a few seconds on a laptop. The figures in this post use the same method on primes up to a trillion, which took 20 minutes on a 64-core cloud machine, about a dollar of compute. The next run I would do is 10¹⁴, which would separate zeros a little higher up for about a hundred times the compute.

Reproducing the spectrum

where Λ(n) is log p when n is a power of a prime p and 0 otherwise, and 1.5772 is one plus Euler’s constant. The prime signal is (E(X) − log 2π)/√X, where the constant log 2π ≈ 1.84 comes from the explicit formula.

import numpy as np

def mangoldt(N):                       # log p at prime powers, else 0
    lam, is_p = np.zeros(N + 1), np.ones(N + 1, bool)
    is_p[:2] = False
    for p in range(2, int(N**0.5) + 1):
        if is_p[p]: is_p[p*p::p] = False
    for p in np.nonzero(is_p)[0]:
        q = p
        while q <= N: lam[q] = np.log(p); q *= p
    return lam

lam = mangoldt(10**7)
n = np.arange(lam.size, dtype=float)
A = np.cumsum(np.divide(lam, n, out=np.zeros_like(lam), where=n > 0))
psi = np.cumsum(lam)
t = np.linspace(np.log(50), np.log(1e7), 2**16)
X = np.exp(t); k = X.astype(int)
E = X*A[k] - psi[k] - X*np.log(X) + (1 + np.euler_gamma)*X
r = (E - np.log(2*np.pi)) / np.sqrt(X)        # the prime signal
w = (r - r.mean()) * np.hanning(t.size)
g = np.linspace(2, 60, 1200)                   # candidate frequencies
P = np.array([abs(np.exp(-1j*f*t) @ w) for f in g])
peaks = P * (0.25 + g**2)                      # peaks sit at the zeros
