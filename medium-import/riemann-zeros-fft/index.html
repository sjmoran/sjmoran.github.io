Finding the Riemann Zeros in a Fourier Transform of the Primes

The number of primes never quite matches its textbook estimate. Treat the mismatch as a time series and its spectrum is the set of zeros of the Riemann zeta function. A data scientist’s route into the Riemann hypothesis and the ideas behind this month’s proof.

Pick a number X and count the primes below it. There is a standard formula that predicts the count, and it is close but never exact: the true count runs a little above the prediction, then a little below, and keeps wobbling as X grows. This week I measured that wobble for every X up to a trillion, treated it as a time series, and ran a Fourier transform over it.

I expected a handful of blurry bumps. Instead the spectrum had 79 sharp peaks below frequency 200 (frequency here means radians per unit of log X). Every one of them sat within 0.03 of a zero of the Riemann zeta function, a function built from the primes that I explain below. (A zero of a function is an input where its output is 0.) And none of the 79 zeros in that range was missing.

Those zeros are the subject of the Riemann hypothesis, which Bernhard Riemann stated in 1859 and which the Clay Mathematics Institute listed in 2000 as one of its seven Millennium Prize Problems. On 20 November 2026 Levent Alpöge announced a proof. He wrote it in Lean, a programming language in which a small trusted program checks every step of a proof, and the next day the maintainers of Lean and its mathematics library, Mathlib, certified it [2].

The proof runs to 17 million lines, which is too long to read. I wanted to see how far a data scientist can get with tools they already use: Fourier transforms, time series and covariance matrices.

Takeaways

  • The error in counting primes is a sum of waves, one per zeta zero, and a Fourier transform recovers the zeros from the primes alone.
  • The Riemann hypothesis says none of those waves grows in size.
  • Proving it comes down to one inequality, because a signal that stays under a fixed ceiling cannot contain a growing wave.
  • Alpöge’s proof gets the inequality from a quantity that can never be negative, sharpened repeatedly with a construction called a theta lift.

The primes make a noisy-looking signal

There are 25 primes below 100, 78,498 below a million and 37,607,912,018 below a trillion. On average the count grows smoothly, and a function called li(X), the logarithmic integral, predicts it well. It is a smoothed version of X / log X, where log is the natural logarithm. Below a million, li(X) is off by 130. Below a trillion it is off by about 38,000, out of 37.6 billion.

A better yardstick is √X. That is the size of error you would expect from adding up X random coin flips, and it comes to 1,000 at a million and 1,000,000 at a trillion. The prime count stays well inside it in both cases, as random noise would.

To look at the error as a signal I made three changes. Each prime p counts as log p instead of 1, which makes the average trend equal to X. The running total is smoothed so that individual jumps average out; the formula for this smoothed error, E(X), is at the end of the post. I subtract a small fixed offset, log 2π ≈ 1.84, that the theory predicts, and I divide the error by √X so it keeps a constant size. And I use t = log X as the time axis. I will call the result the prime signal.

Over 23.7 units of t, from X = 50 to a trillion, the prime signal wiggles around zero with no trend and no growing variance. It never gets above 0.025. The dashed lines show the largest value it could reach if the Riemann hypothesis holds; where that number comes from is explained in the next section.

Each zero of zeta puts one wave into the signal

The zeta function adds up one over every whole number raised to a power s:

Euler showed in 1737 that the same function is a product with one factor for each prime p [4]:

The two forms agree because every whole number splits into primes in exactly one way. Everything zeta does is driven by the primes.

The input s can be a complex number, with a real part and an imaginary part. The sum only adds up to a finite value when the real part of s is above 1. There is exactly one smooth way to extend ζ to every other s, and the zeros live in that extended region. Zeta has some easy zeros at −2, −4, −6 and so on, which only add a small smooth term. The ones that matter all lie in a strip where the real part is between 0 and 1. I will write each one as a + iγ. Its real part a is its position across the strip, and its imaginary part γ is its height.

Each zero contributes one wave to the prime signal. The height γ sets the wave’s frequency: the lowest zero, at height 14.13, gives a wave that repeats every 2π/14.13 ≈ 0.44 units of t. The position a sets its growth, at rate a − ½. A zero at a = ½ gives a wave of constant size, and a zero at a = 0.6 would give one that grows.

If you have designed a filter, this works like one. A filter’s response is set by its poles, the points where its transfer function blows up; each pole adds a resonance with its own frequency and decay. Here the role of the transfer function is played by ζ′/ζ, the derivative of ζ divided by ζ. It blows up exactly at the zeros of ζ, and when you expand it as a sum, its coefficients are the prime weights (log p at each prime p and at its powers). After dividing by √X, the zero at a + iγ contributes exp((a − ½)t) times exp(iγt): a growth factor times a rotating phasor, the unit-length complex number that an FFT uses for each frequency.

Riemann’s explicit formula, an exact identity that writes the prime count as a smooth trend minus a sum over the zeros, turns this into a precise statement [3, 5]. The prime signal is the sum of these waves, one per zero, and the wave from the zero at height γ has amplitude 2/(¼ + γ²):

The formula can be tested from both sides. Adding up the waves from the zeros should rebuild the signal, and it does. With the first three zeros the rebuild already has correlation 0.94 with the real thing; with the first hundred it reaches 0.9998.

Going the other way, the spectrum of the prime signal should peak at every zero’s height, which is what Figure 1 shows. To make the peaks comparable I multiply the spectrum by ¼ + γ², which undoes the shrinking amplitudes. I also apply a Hann window, a taper that fades the series to zero at both ends so that one frequency does not smear into its neighbours. A trillion’s worth of primes gives 23.7 units of t, enough to separate frequencies about 0.5 apart. The closest pair of zeros below 200 is 0.72 apart, at heights 184.87 and 185.60, so each zero gets its own peak.

The hypothesis says no wave grows

The Riemann hypothesis says that every zero has a = ½, so the zeros all sit on one vertical line, the critical line, and every wave in the prime signal keeps the same size forever.

A single zero at a = 0.51 would break this. Its wave would grow slowly, and the prime signal would stop being stationary.

Data alone cannot settle the question, because such a zero could hide for a long time. Put it at height one million. Its wave starts with amplitude about 2 × 10⁻¹² and grows by a factor of e every 100 units of t, so it would not become visible until X reached about 10¹⁰³⁶, a number with over a thousand digits. Platt and Trudgian checked every zero up to height 3 × 10¹², more than ten trillion zeros, and all of them lie on the line [10]. That says nothing about a zero at height 10¹⁵.

A single ceiling would prove it

A wave of constant size spends half its time above zero and half below. A wave whose size keeps growing will, sooner or later, rise above any ceiling you care to draw.

So if you could prove that the prime signal stays below some constant for all large X, no wave in it could be growing, and no zero could have a above ½. Process engineers use the same reasoning with control charts: a metric that never breaches its upper control limit cannot be hiding an oscillation with growing swings. Edmund Landau made the argument rigorous in 1905 [6]. With infinitely many waves in play it needs care, and it relies on the gap between the ceiling and the signal never going negative.

Zeros to the left of the line take care of themselves, because zeros come in mirror pairs across it. A zero at a = 0.3 would have a partner at a = 0.7, and the ceiling has already ruled that one out.

Riemann derived the mirror symmetry, known as the functional equation of zeta, from a sum of Gaussians, using the fact that a Gaussian keeps its shape under the Fourier transform. That sum is called a theta function. The proof uses theta functions again, in the theta lifts described below.

So the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to one ceiling on the prime signal. Figure 2 shows the ceiling holding up to a trillion. The proof has to show it holds for every X beyond some point.

How the proof builds the ceiling

This section is my interpretation, not a check. I read Alpöge’s remark [1] and traced the Lean files down to their main input, which I could not rebuild.

Reduce to the ceiling

The proof works with the same smoothed signal plotted in Figure 2; the Lean files define it with the same formula. It also proves more than the Riemann hypothesis. The same ceiling is established for a whole family of related functions, the Dirichlet L-functions. Each is a version of zeta in which the terms of the zeta sum are multiplied by a repeating pattern of +1, −1 and 0, for example a sign that depends on n modulo 4. The Riemann hypothesis is the simplest member of the family.

Get a weak ceiling from positivity

Quantities that can never be negative are a cheap source of one-sided bounds, and every data scientist has used one. A variance is never negative, so anything you can write as a variance comes with an inequality attached.

A finite version of the problem shows positivity doing this job. Do arithmetic modulo a prime p, like a clock with p hours, and count the solutions (x, y) of an equation such as y² = f(x), where f is a polynomial of degree 5. The solutions trace out what mathematicians call a curve. Repeat the count in larger finite number systems with p², p³ and more elements; these systems are called finite fields. The counts behave like the prime counts, except that they are governed by four complex numbers α₁ to α₄ instead of infinitely many zeros. In the system with p to the power r elements, the count is p to the power r, plus 1, minus the sum of the r-th powers of the α’s. The hypothesis for this setting says each α has absolute value (distance from 0) equal to √p, and André Weil proved it in the 1940s [7].

In this setting the hypothesis is a covariance check. Scale the α’s by √p and add up their r-th powers:

The four α’s come in two complex-conjugate pairs, a + ib and a − ib, so negative powers mirror positive ones and c(−r) = c(r). Suppose each scaled α has absolute value 1, which means it sits on the unit circle in the complex plane. Then c(r) is the autocovariance of a time series made of two sinusoids, and the matrix with c(i − j) in row i and column j is a covariance matrix with no negative eigenvalues. Push an α off the circle and a large enough matrix acquires a negative eigenvalue. Across the nine curves I tried, every matrix up to 29 × 29 had smallest eigenvalue no lower than −2 × 10⁻¹³, which is rounding error. Moving the α’s off the circle by a factor of 1.2 gave −656.

Weil found the same kind of test for the primes [8]. The Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to a certain quadratic form built from the primes never going negative. A quadratic form is an expression like vᵀMv, the same shape as the variance of a portfolio with weights v. There is no finite matrix to check this time, because there are infinitely many zeros. Alpöge’s remark describes proving the positivity for well-chosen test functions, the weighting functions you plug into the quadratic form, “via a determinant”. When a matrix is built from dot products of vectors, its determinant is the squared volume those vectors span, and a squared volume cannot be negative. This positivity yields a weak ceiling, strong enough to rule out zeros with a close to 1.

Sharpen it with multiplicity

The sharpening borrows an idea from the finite version. In 1969 Stepanov gave a proof of the curve case that avoided the algebraic geometry Weil had used [9], and it rests on a budget argument. A polynomial of degree d has room for d roots. Suppose it has a root of multiplicity 10 at every point you are counting, meaning the polynomial and its first nine derivatives are all zero there. Then each point uses up 10 of those roots, so there can be at most d/10 points. The hard part is building a low-degree polynomial that vanishes to high order at every point at once.

According to the remark, the proof gets its multiplicity from theta lifts. Theta functions also exist in many dimensions, as sums of Gaussians over grids of points. A theta lift uses one of them as a kernel K(g, h), in the same sense as the kernel in a kernel method. It turns a function f of h into a function of g:

Here g and h range over groups of symmetries, such as sets of rotations, and the integral averages over every h. Think of it as multiplying a vector by a kernel matrix, carrying a function from one group of symmetries to a larger one. The remark says each lift “wins multiplicity” and that the number of lifts grows with the scale X. In Stepanov’s terms, each lift raises the order to which the auxiliary function behind the positive quantity vanishes, and each extra order tightens the ceiling in the way it tightens Stepanov’s count.

Bootstrap to ½

Each round turns a ceiling into a zero-free region, a band of the strip where no zero can sit. A wider band then gives a sharper ceiling for the next round. The remark calls this loop a bootstrap, and says that repeated rounds push the band all the way to the line a = ½.

What the signal view does not explain

With Fourier transforms and covariance matrices I could follow what the zeros are, what the hypothesis says, why one inequality is enough and, in outline, how the proof produces that inequality. I could not follow why the multiplicity from the theta lifts builds up fast enough, or how the error terms stay under control across so many lifts. Those parts are in the Lean files, and the remark does not say enough to rebuild them.

You can reproduce the spectrum with the code below. It covers primes up to ten million and frequencies up to 60, and runs in a few seconds on a laptop. The trillion-prime figures use the same method spread over 64 cores on a cloud machine, which took 20 minutes and cost about a dollar. The next run I would try is 10¹⁴, which would resolve zeros a little higher up for roughly a hundred times the compute.

Reproducing the spectrum

The smoothed, weighted error is

where Λ(n), the von Mangoldt function, is log p when n is a prime power (p, p², p³ and so on) and 0 otherwise, and 1.5772 is one plus Euler’s constant, 0.5772…, the limit of 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/n − log n. The prime signal is (E(X) − log 2π)/√X; the constant log 2π ≈ 1.84 comes out of the explicit formula.

import numpy as np

def mangoldt(N):                       # log p at prime powers, else 0
    lam, is_p = np.zeros(N + 1), np.ones(N + 1, bool)
    is_p[:2] = False
    for p in range(2, int(N**0.5) + 1):
        if is_p[p]: is_p[p*p::p] = False
    for p in np.nonzero(is_p)[0]:
        q = p
        while q <= N: lam[q] = np.log(p); q *= p
    return lam

lam = mangoldt(10**7)
n = np.arange(lam.size, dtype=float)
A = np.cumsum(np.divide(lam, n, out=np.zeros_like(lam), where=n > 0))
psi = np.cumsum(lam)
t = np.linspace(np.log(50), np.log(1e7), 2**16)
X = np.exp(t); k = X.astype(int)
E = X*A[k] - psi[k] - X*np.log(X) + (1 + np.euler_gamma)*X
r = (E - np.log(2*np.pi)) / np.sqrt(X)        # the prime signal
w = (r - r.mean()) * np.hanning(t.size)
g = np.linspace(2, 60, 1200)                   # candidate frequencies
P = np.array([abs(np.exp(-1j*f*t) @ w) for f in g])
peaks = P * (0.25 + g**2)                      # peaks sit at the zeros
